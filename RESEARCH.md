# Spatial Reasoning at the Margin: A Research Statement

**Wasema Farzana** · Geometry Reader, a Science Readers module

> **Status.** This statement sets out a research agenda. No study has yet been conducted. Every claim about the tool's effects below is a hypothesis, not a finding. The tool is in its first, informal phase of design-based research: it is used in the author's own teaching and revised in response (section 8). That is practice, not a study, and no findings are claimed from it. The field observations are the author's own, gathered through professional practice rather than systematic data collection, and are offered as the empirical motivation for the questions, not as evidence for their answers.

## 1. Positionality: from optimisation to context

I came to anthropology from computer science and engineering. I made that move because the questions I cared about kept falling outside what technical optimisation can answer. Software can be correct, fast and well designed and still fail. It fails if it does not fit the economics of a household, the politics of who may use a device, or the cultural meanings attached to a girl studying mathematics.

Three bodies of experience shape this work:
- **Computing,** which lets me specify and review a working tool rather than only describe one.
- **Five years of teaching,** which supplies the pedagogy (section 4).
- **Development-sector practice, read through anthropology,** which supplies the questions about context: who uses a device, under whose eyes, and at whose cost (section 3).

Geometry Reader is where these meet. It is a working technical artefact, and it is also an instrument for asking anthropological questions about who gets to learn, on whose device, and at what cost.

## 2. The problem: merit as access

In Bangladesh, mathematical "merit" tracks access at least as closely as ability. Access has layers, and software can address only some of them:

| Layer | Question | Can software help? |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Do good resources exist nearby? | Yes: a browser-based resource removes distance. |
| Affordability | Can the household pay for the device and the data, and keep paying? | Partly. |
| Know-how | Does the learner know how to learn with the resource? | Partly. This is a design question. |
| Networks | Does the family know the system and its gatekeepers? | No. |

The layers follow Bourdieu's distinction between economic, cultural and social capital (Bourdieu, 1986). They draw on research into "shadow education", the paid private tutoring that runs alongside schooling and tends to widen existing inequalities (Bray, 1999), and on critiques of meritocracy as a story that credits to talent what access produced (Sandel, 2020).

## 3. Field grounding: the gendered household device

Through extended work in Bangladesh's development sector, I have observed a pattern that the access layers above do not capture: **access to technology is gendered inside the household**.

In the regional peripheries where I worked, a daughter is often framed in household economic reasoning as a cost to be managed rather than an investment to be made. This framing is tied to marriage economics and to long-documented patterns of son preference in South Asia. It shapes access to learning technology in specific ways:

- A girl's use of a shared phone is more often time-limited, supervised and conditional on chores being finished.
- Data spending on her learning competes directly with other household priorities.
- A device used for study is also a device that can be checked, so privacy and learning become entangled.

These dynamics differ markedly from what I have observed in capital-centric (Dhaka) households, where devices are more often individual, data less rationed, and girls' study time less contested. **The gender digital divide at the margin is therefore not only a gap in ownership but a gap in the conditions of use.** These are practitioner observations from one practitioner's work, not population estimates; establishing how general they are is part of the research itself.

## 4. Pedagogical grounding

I have taught for five years, from early-childhood cognitive scaffolding at preschool level to preparation for international O-Level examinations. Two lessons from that range inform the design:

- **Spatial reasoning is built, not revealed.** Young children construct geometric ideas by handling, turning and comparing objects; older learners who missed that stage often meet geometry as text and symbols they must memorise. Much "maths anxiety" in adolescents looks, in practice, like being asked to reason about shapes that were never made tangible.
- **Explanation is not the same as understanding.** Learners with access to private tutoring receive repeated, adaptive explanation; learners at the margin receive a textbook. A tool cannot replace a tutor, but it can make the figure itself do some of the explaining.

Geometry Reader therefore treats every concept as something to manipulate and every theorem as something to *watch* happen on the learner's own figure, with formal vocabulary paired with plain language.

## 5. Where the tool sits in learning

**The gap it is designed for.** School geometry is usually taught in two moves. The textbook states a result, and practice builds fluency in using it. Between the two sits a third move that textbooks rarely make: showing *why* the result holds, until the learner can see it. A good tutor supplies that move by drawing, redrawing and explaining. Families who can pay for tutoring buy it; learners at the margin mostly go without (sections 2 and 4). Geometry Reader is designed to supply that middle move. It is not designed to replace the textbook, practice or the teacher.

**How I use it in my teaching.**
1. **Start with what learners know.** I teach the concept first from their own textbooks and the methods they are used to.
2. **Bring in the tool where confusion remains.** If that explanation leaves a learner confused, Geometry Reader becomes the second explanation. Learners move the figure and watch where a formula comes from and why a result holds. I set out what the tool shows and what it does not.
3. **Practise.** Learners work problems without the tool, so that they connect the ideas themselves.
4. **Observe.** I watch how learners practise and explain: how clear the concept is to them, and how they approach a problem.

Used this way, the tool is a second representation that complements the textbook rather than replacing it. Research on learning with multiple representations sets out why a second form can help: it can show what the first leaves out, constrain how the first is read, and support a deeper understanding than either gives alone (Ainsworth, 2006). In practice I have seen learners become interested in watching the figures once the tool comes in; that is an observation from practice, not a finding.

**What it aims at.** Mathematical proficiency has five intertwined strands: conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, strategic competence, adaptive reasoning and productive disposition (National Research Council, 2001). Geometry Reader targets conceptual understanding and adaptive reasoning, the *what* and the *why*. It may also affect productive disposition, which is what the anxiety hypothesis (H2) tests. Procedural fluency and strategic competence come from practice, which is why practice stays a separate step. In van Hiele's account of geometric thinking (van Hiele, 1986), many learners still judge shapes by how they look, while examinations ask them to reason from properties and to deduce. The tool is aimed at that transition.

**What it changes, and how (hypotheses, not findings):**
- **From one drawing to a family of drawings.** A printed figure shows one case. When the learner drags the figure, what survives every change is what the theorem asserts. Research on dynamic geometry treats dragging in this way, as a route from observing to conjecturing to justifying (Arzarello et al., 2002).
- **From a proof to recite to a proof to watch.** Mathematics education distinguishes proofs that only verify from proofs that explain (Hanna, 2000). The visual proofs are designed to explain: the figure itself moves until the reason is visible.
- **From receiving to acting.** The learner moves the figure rather than reading about it.
- **From jargon to paired language.** Each formal term appears beside a plain-language version, lowering the barrier at the point where many learners freeze.

**An open design question.** In my teaching, the textbook comes first and the tool second, and only where it is needed. Research on *concreteness fading* recommends the reverse: begin with concrete or visual forms and fade towards the abstract (Fyfe et al., 2014). My order is deliberate: learners meet each topic first in the form their examinations use, and the tool repairs what that form leaves unclear. Whether the tool works better as the first explanation or as a repair is a research question (RQ6), not a settled matter. The order also means that Phase 1 sees the tool mostly on the hardest confusions. That is useful for design, but any later comparison must allow for it.

**What it does not supply.** Feedback on a learner's own working, motivation, the accountability a teacher provides, and the social capital a tutor's network brings. General dynamic geometry software, such as GeoGebra, is far more powerful and flexible. Geometry Reader makes a narrower bet: one sequenced path through school geometry, in which every result comes with a visual proof, designed for shared, low-data phones.

## 6. Research questions

The agenda has two tracks, kept apart because they answer different questions for different audiences.

**Track A · Does the tool work?** (learning sciences)
- **RQ1 (Understanding):** Does direct manipulation with visual proofs support conceptual understanding of geometry for learners without access to tutoring?
- **RQ2 (Anxiety):** Does a calm, manipulable, non-evaluative environment reduce mathematics anxiety, and does any effect differ by starting anxiety?
- **RQ6 (Sequencing):** Does the tool work better as the first explanation of a topic, or as a second explanation brought in where the textbook's has left confusion?
- **RQ7 (Language):** Does pairing each formal term with a plain-language translation improve recall and correct use of the formal vocabulary?

**Track B · Gendered access** (part of my wider research on gender, technology and power)
- **RQ3 (Gendered access):** How do the conditions under which girls in regional Bangladesh access shared household devices (time, supervision, data cost) shape whether and how they can use a learning tool like this one?
- **RQ4 (Design for constrained use):** Which design properties (low data cost, no accounts, short self-contained sessions, nothing stored about the learner) matter most to whether such a tool is usable under those conditions?

**A bridge, later**
- **RQ5 (Equity):** Are any benefits distributed differently between girls in regional peripheries and learners in capital-centric settings? This needs the methods of both tracks.

## 7. Hypotheses (untested)

- **H1 (Track A):** Manipulation with visual proof yields larger gains in conceptual understanding than text-first instruction on the same topics.
- **H2 (Track A):** Reductions in mathematics anxiety are largest for learners who start out most anxious.
- **H3 (Track B):** For girls with supervised, time-limited device access, low data cost and the absence of logins and stored data are preconditions for sustained use, not secondary conveniences.
- **H4 (Track A):** Paired vocabulary improves correct use of formal terms without reducing performance on examination-style questions.
- **H5 (Track A):** Watching a shape keep its rules while it is stretched, such as a rectangle becoming a square, improves acceptance of inclusive class relationships ("every square is a rectangle") compared with fixed diagrams.

## 8. Approach: design-based research

Geometry Reader is built and used at the same time. The learning sciences call this **design-based research**: a learning environment is designed for real classrooms, used there, studied and revised in repeated cycles, so that each cycle improves both the design and the theory of how learners learn with it (Brown, 1992; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; Barab & Squire, 2004). In HCI the closely related tradition is **research through design**, in which the artefact itself carries the research contribution (Zimmerman et al., 2007). The project sits within HCI for development, which designs with and for under-served populations (Dell & Kumar, 2016).

**Phase 1, practice (current).** I use the tool in my own teaching (section 5) and revise it in response. Two sources feed each revision:
- **Learners' use:** where learners hesitate, misread a figure, or reach the idea quickly.
- **My own first-person use:** whether a figure makes the idea clear at first sight, and whether the connections show. HCI recognises designing through one's own genuine use as *autobiographical design* (Neustaedter & Sengers, 2012). Its limit is the *expert blind spot*: someone who already knows the mathematics is a poor judge of what a novice will find hard (Nathan & Petrosino, 2003). My own reading therefore tests the clarity of a representation, not its learnability.

I record revisions in a practitioner's design journal, kept offline. In this phase I am designer, teacher and observer at once, so what is seen cannot be separated from the quality of the teaching, from novelty, or from expectation. **Phase 1 therefore produces design decisions, not findings.**

The journal is what makes Phase 1 count:
- it records the reason for every revision, which is the core output of design-based research;
- it shows where learners struggle, which sharpens the research questions and tells Phase 2 what to measure, and on which topics;
- it is the record of how many design cycles the tool has been through.

What it cannot do is show that the tool works.

**Phase 2, studies (planned).** Two separate studies, each with its own ethical approval:
- **Track A** evaluates learning and anxiety outcomes in a small pilot.
- **Track B** studies household device practices through ethnographic and participatory work, set against national survey data. Girls, their families and their teachers are treated as co-designers rather than subjects. The primary population is **female secondary students in regional Bangladesh**, with capital-centric learners as a comparison.

**The strongest counter-argument.** Technology tends to amplify the human capacity already present rather than substitute for what is missing (Toyama, 2015). If that holds here, the tool will help most where good teaching already exists, which is the opposite of its equity aim. RQ5 is designed so that the study can detect this, not only its absence.

## 9. Ethics

Participants are minors. Any study requires institutional ethics approval, parental consent and each student's own assent. The current software collects no personal data and contains no tracking. The Phase 1 journal informs the design and the planning of the study. It is not used as evidence of effect, and notes about individual learners stay offline. Participants, schools, families and specific localities will be anonymised in all outputs.

## 10. What this statement does not claim

- That Geometry Reader improves learning or reduces anxiety. This has not been measured.
- That what I have seen in my own teaching is evidence of effect. It informs the design only.
- That it closes the access gap. At most it narrows the availability and know-how layers.
- That the field observations in section 3 generalise. They motivate RQ3 and RQ5; they do not answer them.
